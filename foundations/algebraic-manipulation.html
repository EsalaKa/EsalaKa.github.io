Algebraic Manipulation

Algebraic manipulation rewrites mathematics into a more useful form without changing the quantity it represents.

Why manipulation matters

The same relationship can be written in several equivalent ways. One form may make calculation easier, another may reveal roots, and another may isolate the quantity we need.

\[\boxed{x^2-5x+6=(x-2)(x-3)}\]

The mathematics has not changed; only our view of it has changed. This idea is used constantly in calculus and mathematical modelling.

1. Simplifying like terms

Like terms have the same variable part:

\[3x+5x=8x,\qquad4x^2-2x^2=2x^2.\]

But \(x\) and \(x^2\) cannot be combined because they represent different powers.

2. Expanding brackets

The distributive law

\[a(b+c)=ab+ac\]

shows that a multiplier outside a bracket acts on every term inside it.

3. Expanding two brackets

\[(x+2)(x+3)=x^2+5x+6.\]

Each term in one bracket multiplies each term in the other.

4. Factorising

Factorising reverses expansion:

\[6x+9=3(2x+3).\]

It exposes common factors and often makes equations or expressions easier to analyse.

5. Rearranging formulas

If \(y=3x+5\), we can isolate \(x\):

\[x=\frac{y-5}{3}.\]

This is essential when a model gives one variable in terms of another but we need to solve for a different quantity.

6. Algebraic fractions

They obey ordinary fraction rules:

\[\frac1x+\frac1y=\frac{x+y}{xy},\qquad x,y\ne0.\]

Restrictions such as non-zero denominators are part of the mathematics and must not be lost during simplification.

7. Cancelling means cancelling factors

We may simplify

\[\frac{x(x+2)}x=x+2,\qquad x\ne0,\]

because \(x\) is a common factor. But \((x+2)/x\) cannot be simplified by cancelling \(x\), because addition separates terms, not factors.

8. Choosing the useful form

Expanded, factorised and rearranged forms can all be correct. Good algebra means choosing the form that makes the next mathematical step clearest.
