Algebraic Expressions

Algebra uses symbols to describe quantities generally, so one mathematical expression can represent many different numerical situations.

Why algebraic expressions matter

Arithmetic answers a particular calculation. Algebra lets us describe the rule behind the calculation. For example, if a taxi costs £3 plus £2 for each mile, the total cost for \(x\) miles is \(3+2x\). The same expression works for every journey length.

\[\boxed{\text{numbers describe one case}\qquad\text{algebra describes a general relationship}}\]

This ability to represent changing quantities is essential for formulas, functions, graphs, scientific models and calculus.

1. Variables

A variable is a symbol representing a number that may change or may not yet be known.

\[x,\qquad y,\qquad t\]

In \(x+4\), the value of the expression depends on \(x\). In applications, \(x\) might represent distance, time, population or another measurable quantity.

2. Constants

A constant is a fixed value. In

\[3x+5,\]

the number \(5\) is constant. Constants represent quantities that remain fixed while variables change.

3. Coefficients

A coefficient multiplies a variable. In \(7x\), the coefficient is \(7\). It often tells us how strongly one quantity depends on another. If no number is written, the coefficient is \(1\):

\[x=1x.\]

4. Terms

Terms are parts separated by \(+\) or \(-\). For example,

\[4x^2-3x+6\]

has the terms \(4x^2\), \(-3x\) and \(6\). Recognising terms helps us simplify and manipulate formulas correctly.

5. Evaluating an expression

An expression represents a value rather than making a true-or-false statement. If

\[2x+1\]

and \(x=3\), then

\[2(3)+1=7.\]

Substitution connects a general formula to a particular situation.

6. Expression versus equation

An expression such as \(3x+2\) represents a quantity. An equation such as

\[3x+2=11\]

states that two quantities are equal. This distinction becomes important when solving equations and defining functions.
