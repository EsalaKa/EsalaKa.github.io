FOUNDATIONS 04

Inequalities

Inequalities describe limits and ranges rather than one exact value.

Why inequalities matter

Real problems often ask for an acceptable range rather than an exact answer. A bridge may support loads below a limit, a medicine may be used within a dose range, or a model may require a population to remain above zero.

\[\boxed{\text{equation: exact equality}\qquad\text{inequality: allowed range}}\]

1. The main symbols

\[\lt,\qquad\gt,\qquad\le,\qquad\ge\]

For example, \(x\le4\) means that \(x\) may be any value below or equal to \(4\).

2. Ranges of values

The inequality \(x\gt2\) represents infinitely many possible real values: \(2.1,3,10\), and every other number greater than \(2\). This makes inequalities useful for constraints, domains and error bounds.

3. Adding or subtracting

Adding the same quantity to both sides preserves order:

\[a\lt b\Longrightarrow a+c\lt b+c.\]

Thus \(x+3\lt8\) gives \(x\lt5\).

4. Multiplication by a positive number

If \(c\gt0\),

\[a\lt b\Longrightarrow ac\lt bc.\]

Positive scaling stretches or shrinks the number line without reversing its direction.

5. Why a negative multiplier reverses the sign

Multiplication by \(-1\) reflects the number line about zero. For example, \(2\lt5\), but \(-2\gt-5\). Therefore

\[a\lt b\Longrightarrow -a\gt-b.\]

This is why dividing \(-2x\lt6\) by \(-2\) gives \(x\gt-3\).

6. Double inequalities

\(2\lt x\lt5\) means that \(x\) is simultaneously greater than \(2\) and less than \(5\). It describes a region of the number line rather than a single point.

7. Interval notation

The same range can be written

\[2\lt x\lt5\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(2,5),\]

while

\[2\le x\le5\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad[2,5].\]

Round brackets exclude an endpoint; square brackets include it. Interval notation becomes important for domains, limits and analysis.
