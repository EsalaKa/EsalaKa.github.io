FOUNDATIONS 09

Mathematical Notation

Mathematical notation is a compact language. Learning to read its structure is as important as learning the calculations themselves.

Why notation matters

A short expression can contain an entire mathematical idea. For example, \(f(x)\), \(\Delta x\), \(x^2\) and \(x_1\) all use the same letter but communicate very different information.

\[\boxed{\text{symbol}\rightarrow\text{structure}\rightarrow\text{meaning}}\]

Clear notation allows mathematics to be communicated precisely across subjects and languages. Misreading one symbol can change the whole meaning of a calculation.

1. Variables

Letters such as \(x,y,t,n\) represent quantities. Their meaning comes from their definition; \(t\) often means time, but mathematics does not require it to.

2. Equality and inequality

\(=\) means equal value. The symbols \(<,>,\le,\ge\) describe order. These symbols express relationships rather than operations.

3. Brackets

Brackets show which parts belong together. \(2(x+3)\) means that \(2\) multiplies the whole quantity \(x+3\), so structure determines the order of calculation.

4. Superscripts

A superscript often represents a power:

\[x^2=x\times x.\]

Later superscripts can have other meanings, so context always matters.

5. Subscripts

Subscripts usually label quantities:

\[x_1,\quad x_2,\quad x_3.\]

They let us distinguish several related values without inventing a new letter for each one.

6. Fractions

\(a/b\) means division, with \(b\ne0\). The fraction bar also groups expressions:

\[\frac{x+1}{x-1}.\]

The entire numerator is divided by the entire denominator.

7. Sets

Braces describe a collection, such as \(A=\{1,2,3\}\). The notation \(2\in A\) means that \(2\) belongs to \(A\), while \(\mathbb N\subset\mathbb R\) expresses containment between sets.

8. Greek letters

Symbols such as \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\Delta,\pi\) are simply additional mathematical letters. Their meaning depends on context. For example, \(\Delta x\) commonly means finite change in \(x\).

9. Function notation

\(f(x)\), read “\(f\) of \(x\),” means the output of function \(f\) at input \(x\). If \(f(x)=x^2+1\), then \(f(3)=10\). The brackets here indicate an input, not multiplication.

10. Similar symbols can mean different things

\(\Delta x\) represents a finite change. Later, \(dx\) appears in differential and integral notation. They are related to change but are not interchangeable symbols.

11. Read mathematics in layers

Rather than reading only left to right, identify the structure:

\[x\longrightarrow x+1\longrightarrow(x+1)^2\longrightarrow\frac{(x+1)^2}{3}.\]

This habit becomes increasingly important when formulas contain derivatives, integrals, vectors and stochastic notation.

12. Where foundations lead

\[\boxed{\text{numbers}\rightarrow\text{algebra}\rightarrow\text{graphs}\rightarrow\text{change}\rightarrow\text{calculus}}\]

The purpose of these foundations is not to memorise isolated rules. They provide the language needed to understand functions, limits, derivatives and integrals.
