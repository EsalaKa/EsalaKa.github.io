FOUNDATIONS 04

Inequalities

Inequalities describe order, limits and ranges of possible values rather than requiring two quantities to be exactly equal.

The main idea

An equation such as \(x=4\) identifies one exact value. An inequality such as \(x\le4\) describes an entire range of values.

\[\boxed{\text{equation: exact value}\qquad\text{inequality: range of values}}\]

This makes inequalities natural for constraints: a temperature below a limit, a positive population, an acceptable error, or the domain on which a function is defined.

1. The inequality symbols

The four basic symbols compare two quantities.

\(a<b\)
a is less than b
\(a>b\)
a is greater than b
\(a\le b\)
a is at most b
\(a\ge b\)
a is at least b

2. An inequality represents a region

The inequality \(x>2\) means every real number to the right of \(2\), but not \(2\) itself.

The open circle shows that the endpoint is excluded.

3. Including an endpoint

For \(x\le4\), all values to the left of \(4\) are allowed, including \(4\).

A filled point shows that the endpoint is included.

4. Adding or subtracting preserves order

Adding the same number to both sides shifts both quantities equally, so their order does not change.

\[a<b\Longrightarrow a+c<b+c.\]

Thus \(x+3<8\) gives \(x<5\).

\(x+3<8\)
\(x+3-3<8-3\)
\(\boxed{x<5}\)

5. Multiplying by a positive number preserves order

If \(c>0\), then

\[a<b\Longrightarrow ac<bc.\]

Positive scaling stretches or shrinks the number line without reversing left and right.

\(2<5\)
multiply by \(3\)
\(6<15\)

6. Multiplying by a negative number reverses order

Multiplication by \(-1\) reflects numbers across zero. Since \(2<5\), after reflection we have \(-2>-5\).

Therefore the inequality sign must reverse when multiplying or dividing by a negative number.

\(-2x<6\)
divide by \(-2\)
\(\boxed{x>-3}\)

7. Double inequalities

The statement \(2<x<5\) means that both conditions hold at once:

\[x>2\qquad\text{and}\qquad x<5.\]

So \(x\) lies strictly between \(2\) and \(5\).

8. Interval notation

Intervals give a compact way to write ranges of real numbers.

\[2<x<5\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\in(2,5),\]\[2\le x\le5\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x\in[2,5].\]

Round brackets exclude an endpoint; square brackets include it.

\((2,5)\): endpoints excluded
\([2,5]\): endpoints included
\([2,5)\): 2 included, 5 excluded
Key takeaways

Inequalities describe ordered ranges. Addition and subtraction preserve the inequality direction. Multiplication or division by a positive number also preserves it, while multiplication or division by a negative number reverses it. Open and closed endpoints distinguish strict from inclusive bounds.

Why this matters for calculus

Calculus frequently works on intervals and constrained domains. Inequalities describe where functions exist, where derivatives are positive or negative, where a function increases or decreases, and how close a variable must be to a point in the precise definition of a limit.
