CHANGE AND LIMITS 03

Average Rate of Change

Average rate of change compares how much the output changes with how much the input changes.

The main idea

Suppose \(x\) changes by \(\Delta x\) and, as a result, \(y\) changes by \(\Delta y\).

The average rate of change is

\[ \boxed{ \text{average rate of change} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} } \]

It tells us the amount of change in \(y\) per unit change in \(x\).

1. A simple example

Suppose

\[ x:2\rightarrow5 \]

and

\[ y:4\rightarrow10. \]

Then

\[ \Delta x=5-2=3 \]

and

\[ \Delta y=10-4=6. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac63 = 2. \]

On average, \(y\) increases by \(2\) units for each unit increase in \(x\).

2. For a function

Suppose

\[ y=f(x). \]

Between the inputs \(x_1\) and \(x_2\),

\[ \Delta x=x_2-x_1 \]

and

\[ \Delta y=f(x_2)-f(x_1). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x_2)-f(x_1)} {x_2-x_1} } \]

3. Example with a curved function

Consider

\[ f(x)=x^2. \]

Find the average rate of change from

\[ x=1 \]

to

\[ x=3. \]

The corresponding outputs are

\[ f(1)=1 \]

and

\[ f(3)=9. \]

Therefore,

\[ \Delta x=3-1=2 \]

and

\[ \Delta y=9-1=8. \]

Hence,

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac82 = 4. \]

4. What does the value \(4\) mean?

It does not mean that the slope of \(y=x^2\) is always \(4\).

It means that between \(x=1\) and \(x=3\), the output changes by an average of \(4\) units for each unit change in \(x\).

A curved function can have different average rates over different intervals.

5. The geometric meaning

On the graph of \(y=f(x)\), consider the two points

\[ P=(x_1,f(x_1)) \]

and

\[ Q=(x_2,f(x_2)). \]

The gradient of the straight line joining \(P\) and \(Q\) is

\[ \frac{f(x_2)-f(x_1)} {x_2-x_1}. \]

This straight line is called a secant line.

\[ \boxed{ \text{average rate of change} = \text{gradient of the secant line} } \]

6. Another useful form

Instead of writing the second input as \(x_2\), we can write it as

\[ x+\Delta x. \]

Then the two outputs are

\[ f(x) \]

and

\[ f(x+\Delta x). \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)} {\Delta x} } \]

This expression is called a difference quotient.

7. Example with \(f(x)=x^2\)

For

\[ f(x)=x^2, \]

the difference quotient is

\[ \frac{(x+\Delta x)^2-x^2} {\Delta x}. \]

Expanding,

\[ = \frac{x^2+2x\Delta x+(\Delta x)^2-x^2} {\Delta x}. \]

So, for \(\Delta x\neq0\),

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = 2x+\Delta x. \]

8. Making the interval smaller

Suppose we begin at \(x=2\). Then

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = 4+\Delta x. \]

Now make \(\Delta x\) smaller:

\[ \begin{array}{c|c} \Delta x & 4+\Delta x\\ \hline 1 & 5\\ 0.5 & 4.5\\ 0.1 & 4.1\\ 0.01 & 4.01\\ 0.001 & 4.001 \end{array} \]

The average rate gets closer and closer to

\[ 4. \]

9. The question that creates calculus

Average rate of change uses two different points.

But what if we want the rate of change at exactly one point?

We cannot simply put

\[ \Delta x=0 \]

into

\[ \frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)} {\Delta x}, \]

because that would produce division by zero.

Instead, calculus studies what happens as

\[ \Delta x\rightarrow0. \]

The two points move closer together, and the secant line approaches a tangent line. This is where the idea of a limit becomes essential.
