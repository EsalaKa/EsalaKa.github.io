FUNCTIONS 13

Transformations of Functions

Transformations move, stretch, compress or reflect a graph while preserving its underlying structure.

The main idea

Starting with \(y=f(x)\), a transformed function can often be written as

\[\boxed{y=A f(B(x-C))+D}.\]

The constants outside \(f\) act vertically; the constants inside \(f\) act horizontally.

1. Vertical translation

\(y=f(x)+a\) moves the graph vertically. Compare \(y=x^2\) and \(y=x^2+3\).

2. Horizontal translation

\(y=f(x-a)\) shifts the graph right by \(a\). Thus \(y=(x-2)^2\) has vertex \((2,0)\).

3. Vertical stretching and compression

\(y=Af(x)\) multiplies every vertical distance by \(|A|\).

4. Reflection in the \(x\)-axis

\(y=-f(x)\) changes each point \((x,y)\) into \((x,-y)\).

5. Reflection in the \(y\)-axis

\(y=f(-x)\) changes each point \((x,y)\) into \((-x,y)\).

6. Horizontal stretching and compression

For \(y=f(Bx)\), the horizontal scale factor is \(1/|B|\). Compare \(\sin x\) and \(\sin(2x)\).

7. Several transformations together

For \(y=2(x-3)^2+1\), shift right \(3\), stretch vertically by \(2\), then shift up \(1\). The vertex is \((3,1)\).

8. Why inside transformations look reversed

For \(y=f(x-2)\), the original input \(0\) occurs when \(x-2=0\), so the feature moves to \(x=2\).

9. Reading the general transformation

In \(y=A f(B(x-C))+D\), \(A\) controls vertical scale/reflection, \(B\) horizontal scale/reflection, \(C\) horizontal shift, and \(D\) vertical shift.

\(A\): vertical
\(B\): horizontal scale
\(C\): horizontal shift
\(D\): vertical shift

10. Why transformations matter for calculus

Transformations help us predict how parameters alter roots, turning points and rates of change.

Key takeaways

Outside changes act vertically and inside changes act horizontally. Translations move graphs, scale factors stretch or compress them, and negative factors produce reflections.
