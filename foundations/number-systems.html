FOUNDATIONS 01

Number Systems

Different mathematical problems require different kinds of numbers. Number systems extend as soon as an earlier system can no longer represent what we need.

The main idea

We begin with numbers for counting. We then need negative numbers, fractions and finally numbers such as \(\sqrt2\) and \(\pi\). Each extension keeps the numbers we already had and adds new ones.

\[\boxed{\mathbb N\subset\mathbb Z\subset\mathbb Q\subset\mathbb R}\]

This tells us which systems contain which. The number line gives a different view: it shows where the numbers actually lie.

1. Numbers on the real number line

Every point on the ordinary number line represents exactly one real number. Integers are only some of these points; fractions and irrational numbers lie between them.

The positions are numerical, not decorative. For example, \(\sqrt2\approx1.414\), so it lies between \(1\) and \(2\), while \(\pi\approx3.142\) lies just to the right of \(3\).

2. Natural numbers \(\mathbb N\)

Natural numbers are used for counting whole objects:

\[\mathbb N=\{1,2,3,4,\ldots\}.\]

Some definitions also include \(0\). They are appropriate for quantities such as 25 students or 8 books, but they cannot represent debt, half an object, or a continuous measurement.

3. Integers \(\mathbb Z\)

To represent zero and values below zero, we extend to the integers:

\[\mathbb Z=\{\ldots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\ldots\}.\]

Integers are useful for temperatures below zero, gains and losses, floors below ground level and signed changes. On the number line they occur at whole-number positions.

4. Rational numbers \(\mathbb Q\)

Integers are still not enough when quantities must be divided. A rational number can be written as

\[\frac pq,\qquad p,q\in\mathbb Z,\qquad q\ne0.\]

Examples include \(1/2\), \(-3/4\), \(2.5=5/2\), and every integer because, for example, \(5=5/1\).

Rational numbers are needed for proportions, probabilities, rates, money and measurements. They fill many points between the integers; there are infinitely many rational numbers between any two distinct real numbers.

5. Rational decimals

A rational number may appear as a fraction, a terminating decimal or a repeating decimal:

\[0.5=\frac12,\qquad 0.75=\frac34,\qquad0.333\ldots=\frac13.\]

The notation changes, but the position on the number line does not. For example, \(3/4\) and \(0.75\) are the same point.

6. Irrational numbers

Some exact quantities cannot be written as a fraction of two integers. These are irrational numbers. Examples include

\[\sqrt2,\qquad\pi,\qquad e.\]

Their decimal expansions neither terminate nor eventually repeat. They are not “outside” the number line: they occupy ordinary points on it, mixed among the rational numbers.

They arise naturally in mathematics: \(\sqrt2\) is the diagonal length of a unit square, \(\pi\) appears in circles, and \(e\) appears in continuous growth and calculus.

7. Real numbers \(\mathbb R\)

Real numbers consist of all rational and irrational numbers:

\[\mathbb R=\mathbb Q\cup(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q).\]

So the complete ordinary number line is the real-number system. Every point corresponds to a real number, and every real number has a position on the line.

8. Two ways to understand the systems

The containment relation

\[\mathbb N\subset\mathbb Z\subset\mathbb Q\subset\mathbb R\]

tells us that every natural number is an integer, every integer is rational, and every rational number is real.

The number line tells us something different: integers, fractions and irrational numbers are all locations on the same continuous real line.

\[\boxed{\text{counting}\rightarrow\text{signed values}\rightarrow\text{fractions}\rightarrow\text{continuous real quantities}}\]

9. Why this matters for calculus

Calculus studies quantities that can vary continuously. A variable such as time or distance may move through values such as \(1\), \(1.1\), \(1.01\), \(\sqrt2\), and every real value between them.

This is why the real number line becomes the natural setting for functions, limits, derivatives and integrals.
