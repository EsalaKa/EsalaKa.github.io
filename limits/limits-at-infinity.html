Limits at Infinity

Limits at infinity describe the long-run behaviour of a function as the input becomes very large positive or negative.

The main idea

The notation

\[\boxed{\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=L}\]

means that \(f(x)\) approaches \(L\) as \(x\) increases without bound.

1. A simple example

For \(f(x)=1/x\),

\[\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac1x=0.\]

The function gets closer to the \(x\)-axis as \(x\) grows.

2. Horizontal asymptotes

If \(f(x)\to L\) as \(x\to\infty\) or \(x\to-\infty\), then \(y=L\) is a horizontal asymptote in that direction.

3. Rational functions with equal degrees

For

\[f(x)=\frac{2x+1}{x+1},\]

divide numerator and denominator by \(x\):

\[\frac{2+1/x}{1+1/x}\to2.\]

Therefore \(\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=2\).

4. Comparing polynomial degrees

For rational functions, the highest powers control long-run behaviour. If the denominator degree is larger, the limit is often \(0\). If the degrees are equal, the limit is the ratio of leading coefficients.

denominator degree larger → \(0\)

equal degrees → ratio of leading coefficients

numerator degree larger → inspect growth

5. Positive and negative infinity can differ

Long-run behaviour should be checked in both directions when relevant:

\[\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x),\qquad\lim_{x\to-\infty}f(x).\]

Some functions have the same end behaviour; others do not.

Key takeaways

Limits at infinity describe end behaviour rather than behaviour near a finite point. They are closely connected to horizontal asymptotes and can often be evaluated by comparing dominant terms.
