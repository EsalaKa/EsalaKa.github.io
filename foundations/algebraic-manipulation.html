FOUNDATIONS 06

Algebraic Manipulation

Algebraic manipulation changes the form of an expression without changing its value.

The main idea

Two expressions can look different but represent the same quantity.

\[ 2(x+3)=2x+6 \]

Algebraic manipulation rewrites expressions into forms that are more useful.

1. Simplifying like terms

Like terms have the same variable part.

\[ 3x+5x=8x. \]

Similarly,

\[ 4x^2-2x^2=2x^2. \]

But \(x\) and \(x^2\) are different kinds of terms and cannot be combined.

2. Expanding brackets

The distributive law is

\[ a(b+c)=ab+ac. \]

For example,

\[ 3(x+4)=3x+12. \]

The multiplier outside the bracket acts on every term inside it.

3. Expanding two brackets

Each term in one bracket multiplies each term in the other.

\[ (x+2)(x+3) \]

gives

\[ x^2+3x+2x+6 \]

and therefore

\[ x^2+5x+6. \]

4. Factorising

Factorising reverses expansion.

For example,

\[ 6x+9 \]

has a common factor of \(3\), so

\[ 6x+9=3(2x+3). \]

The factorised and expanded forms represent the same expression.

5. Rearranging equations

Rearranging an equation means rewriting it so that a chosen variable is isolated.

Suppose

\[ y=3x+5. \]

Subtract \(5\):

\[ y-5=3x. \]

Then divide by \(3\):

\[ x=\frac{y-5}{3}. \]

The relationship has not changed; only its form has changed.

6. Fractions in algebra

Algebraic fractions follow the same rules as numerical fractions.

For example,

\[ \frac{x}{3}+\frac{2x}{3} = \frac{3x}{3} = x. \]

When denominators differ, a common denominator is needed.

\[ \frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{y} = \frac{x+y}{xy}, \]

provided \(x\neq0\) and \(y\neq0\).

7. Cancelling factors

Cancellation is valid only when the same non-zero factor multiplies both numerator and denominator.

\[ \frac{3x}{3}=x. \]

Similarly,

\[ \frac{x(x+2)}{x}=x+2, \qquad x\neq0. \]

But terms separated by addition cannot be cancelled.

\[ \frac{x+2}{x} \]

does not simplify by cancelling the \(x\).

8. Why equivalent forms matter

Different forms are useful for different purposes.

For example,

\[ x^2-5x+6 \]

and

\[ (x-2)(x-3) \]

are equivalent.

The expanded form shows the polynomial terms clearly, while the factorised form makes its roots easier to see.
