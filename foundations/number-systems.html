FOUNDATIONS 01

Number Systems

Different mathematical problems require different kinds of numbers.

The main idea

Number systems are not separate collections with no connection. They grow from one another.

\[ \mathbb N \subset \mathbb Z \subset \mathbb Q \subset \mathbb R \]

The symbol \(\subset\) means is contained in.

So every natural number is an integer, every integer is rational, and every rational number is real.

01

Natural numbers

Natural numbers are used for counting:

\[ 1,\;2,\;3,\;4,\ldots \]

We write

\[ \mathbb N=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}. \]

Some definitions include \(0\) in \(\mathbb N\), so the convention should be stated when it matters.

02

Integers

Counting numbers are not enough when zero and negative values are needed.

Adding them gives the integers:

\[ \mathbb Z = \{\ldots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\ldots\}. \]

Therefore,

\[ \mathbb N\subset\mathbb Z. \]
03

Rational numbers

Integers are not enough when division produces parts of a whole.

A rational number is any number that can be written as

\[ \frac{p}{q}, \qquad p,q\in\mathbb Z, \qquad q\neq0. \]

For example,

\[ \frac12, \qquad -\frac34, \qquad 5=\frac51. \]

The important point is that a rational number does not have to look like a fraction. It only needs to be possible to write it as one.

04

What about decimals?

A decimal may also be rational.

For example,

\[ 0.5=\frac12 \]

and

\[ 0.333\ldots=\frac13. \]

Terminating decimals and repeating decimals are rational numbers.

05

Irrational numbers

Some real numbers cannot be written as a fraction of two integers. These are called irrational numbers.

Examples are

\[ \sqrt2,\qquad \pi,\qquad e. \]

Their decimal expansions continue indefinitely without eventually repeating a fixed pattern.

06

Real numbers

The real numbers contain both rational and irrational numbers.

\[ \mathbb R = \{\text{rational numbers}\} \cup \{\text{irrational numbers}\}. \]

Every point on the ordinary number line corresponds to a real number.

A number can belong to several systems

Consider \(3\).

It is a natural number:

\[ 3\in\mathbb N. \]

It is therefore also an integer.

\[ 3\in\mathbb Z. \]

And because

\[ 3=\frac31, \]

it is rational, and therefore real.

\[ 3\in\mathbb N, \qquad 3\in\mathbb Z, \qquad 3\in\mathbb Q, \qquad 3\in\mathbb R. \]

The symbol \(\in\) means is an element of.

Two symbols that look similar

These statements mean different things:

\[ 3\in\mathbb N \]

means

3 is an element of the natural numbers.

But

\[ \mathbb N\subset\mathbb Z \]

means

the whole set of natural numbers is contained in the integers.

So \(\in\) compares an element with a set, while \(\subset\) compares one set with another.

Why real numbers matter for calculus

Calculus studies continuous change.

The real number line has no gaps. Between any two different real numbers, another real number can always be found.

For example, between \(1\) and \(2\) lies

\[ 1.5. \]

Between \(1\) and \(1.5\) lies

\[ 1.25. \]

We can continue this process no matter how close the two numbers become.

This ability to approach values as closely as we wish is fundamental to the idea of a limit, and limits are the foundation of calculus.

\[ \mathbb R \longrightarrow \text{continuous values} \longrightarrow \text{limits} \longrightarrow \frac{dy}{dx}. \]

The whole picture

\[ \boxed{\mathbb N} \subset \boxed{\mathbb Z} \subset \boxed{\mathbb Q} \subset \boxed{\mathbb R} \]

Each new system keeps the previous numbers and adds numbers needed to solve problems that the smaller system cannot handle.
