CHANGE AND LIMITS 02

Finite Change

Before taking a limit, calculus begins with an ordinary non-zero interval between two points on a function.

The main idea

For \(y=f(x)\), start at the point

\[A=(x,f(x))\]

and move to

\[B=(x+\Delta x,f(x+\Delta x)).\]

The finite input and output changes are

\[\boxed{\Delta x},\qquad\boxed{\Delta y=f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}.\]

1. Two points define a finite change

Take \(f(x)=x^2\), with

\[A=(1,1),\qquad B=(2,4).\]

Then

\[\Delta x=1,\qquad\Delta y=3.\]

The graph shows the horizontal change \(\Delta x\) and vertical change \(\Delta y\) between the two points.

2. Writing the second point using \(\Delta x\)

If the first input is \(x\), then the second input can be written

\[\boxed{x+\Delta x}.\]

The corresponding points on the curve are therefore

\[(x,f(x))\quad\text{and}\quad(x+\Delta x,f(x+\Delta x)).\]
first point
\((x,f(x))\)

move horizontally by \(\Delta x\)

second point
\((x+\Delta x,f(x+\Delta x))\)

3. The corresponding output change

The vertical difference between the two points is

\[\boxed{\Delta y=f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}.\]

This formula is important because it expresses the change in the function entirely in terms of the starting input and its increment.

4. Example with \(f(x)=x^2\)

For a general starting value \(x\),

\[\Delta y=(x+\Delta x)^2-x^2.\]

Expanding gives

\[\boxed{\Delta y=2x\Delta x+(\Delta x)^2}.\]

So the output change depends both on where we start and on the width of the interval.

\((x+\Delta x)^2-x^2\)
↓ expand
\(x^2+2x\Delta x+(\Delta x)^2-x^2\)
↓
\(2x\Delta x+(\Delta x)^2\)

5. Finite change gives a secant line

The two distinct points \(A\) and \(B\) determine a straight line called a secant line.

Its slope is

\[\boxed{\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}}.\]

This is the average rate of change of the function across the finite interval.

6. The secant slope for \(x^2\)

Using

\[\Delta y=2x\Delta x+(\Delta x)^2,\]

and \(\Delta x\ne0\),

\[\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\frac{2x\Delta x+(\Delta x)^2}{\Delta x}=\boxed{2x+\Delta x}.\]

The slope therefore changes as the interval width changes.

7. Making the finite interval smaller

Fix the starting point at \(x=1\). Then the secant slope is

\[2+\Delta x.\]

For example,

\[\Delta x=1\Rightarrow3,\qquad\Delta x=0.5\Rightarrow2.5,\qquad\Delta x=0.1\Rightarrow2.1.\]

The graph can show the second point moving toward the first and the secant line approaching a limiting position.

8. Finite change is not yet the derivative

For every finite interval, the two points are still distinct:

\[\boxed{\Delta x\ne0}.\]

Therefore \(\Delta y/\Delta x\) is a secant slope, not yet the instantaneous slope at one point.

finite \(\Delta x\)
→ two distinct points
→ secant line
→ average rate of change

9. The transition to a limit

Calculus now asks what value the secant slope approaches as

\[\Delta x\to0.\]

For \(f(x)=x^2\),

\[2x+\Delta x\longrightarrow2x.\]

This limiting process turns finite change into instantaneous rate of change.

↓ shrink \(\Delta x\)
secant line
↓ limit

↓
derivative
Key takeaways

A finite change compares two distinct points on a function. The horizontal difference is \(\Delta x\), the vertical difference is \(\Delta y=f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)\), and their ratio is the slope of the secant line. Shrinking this finite interval leads naturally to limits and derivatives.
