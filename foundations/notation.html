FOUNDATIONS 09

Mathematical Notation

Mathematical notation is a compact language. The aim is not only to recognise symbols, but to read the structure and meaning of a mathematical statement.

The main idea

A small change in notation can produce a completely different mathematical object. For example, \(x^2\), \(x_2\), \(f(x)\), \(\Delta x\) and \(dx\) all contain \(x\), but they do not mean the same thing.

\[\boxed{\text{symbol}\longrightarrow\text{structure}\longrightarrow\text{meaning}}\]

Good notation lets complicated ideas be expressed precisely and compactly.

1. Variables and constants

Letters such as \(x\), \(y\) and \(t\) often represent quantities that may vary. A constant has a fixed value within the problem.

The letter itself does not determine the meaning: \(t\) often represents time only because we define it that way.

\(x=\text{position}\)
\(t=\text{time}\)
\(g=9.81\text{ m/s}^2\)

2. Equality, approximation and inequality

Relationship symbols say how two mathematical quantities are connected.

\(a=b\)
equal
\(a\approx b\)
approximately equal
\(a<b\)
less than
\(a\ge b\)
greater than or equal

3. Brackets show structure

Brackets tell us which quantities belong together. They can change the value of an expression.

\(2(x+3)=2x+6\)
but
\(2x+3\) is different

4. Superscripts

A superscript commonly represents a power:

\[x^3=x\cdot x\cdot x.\]

But superscripts can later label derivatives, inverses or components, so their meaning must always be read from context.

\(x^2\): square of \(x\)
\(x^{-1}\): reciprocal of \(x\)
\(f^{-1}\): inverse function, not \(1/f\)

5. Subscripts

Subscripts usually label related quantities rather than indicating multiplication.

\[x_1,\quad x_2,\quad x_3.\]

For example, \(x_1\) may mean the first value of \(x\), while \(x_2\) means the second.

\(x_1=2\)
\(x_2=5\)
\(\Delta x=x_2-x_1=3\)

6. Fractions group numerator and denominator

The fraction bar means division, but it also acts like brackets around the entire numerator and denominator.

\[\frac{x+1}{x-1}.\]

This means \((x+1)\div(x-1)\), not \(x+1\div x-1\).

numerator: \(x+1\)
────────
denominator: \(x-1\)
restriction: \(x\ne1\)

7. Sets and membership

Braces can describe a set. The symbol \(\in\) means “is an element of”, while \(\subset\) describes containment of one set inside another.

\(A=\{1,2,3\}\)
\(2\in A\)
\(5\notin A\)
\(\mathbb N\subset\mathbb R\)

8. Greek letters

Greek letters provide additional mathematical symbols. They are not special operations by themselves; their meaning comes from their definition.

\(\alpha\) — alpha
\(\beta\) — beta
\(\gamma\) — gamma
\(\Delta\) — capital delta
\(\theta\) — theta
\(\pi\) — pi

9. Function notation

\(f(x)\), read “\(f\) of \(x\)”, means the output of function \(f\) when the input is \(x\). The brackets do not mean multiplication.

\(f(x)=x^2+1\)
input \(x=3\)
\(f(3)=3^2+1=10\)

10. Finite-change notation

\(\Delta x\) means a finite change in \(x\):

\[\Delta x=x_2-x_1.\]

Similarly, \(\Delta y=y_2-y_1\). The ratio \(\Delta y/\Delta x\) is an average rate of change.

\(x_1\to x_2\)
\(\Delta x=x_2-x_1\)
\(\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\text{average rate}\)

11. Differential notation

Later in calculus we meet \(dx\), \(dy\) and

\[\frac{dy}{dx}.\]

These are related to change, but \(dx\) is not simply another spelling of \(\Delta x\). Their precise meaning develops from limits and differentials.

\(\Delta x\): finite change
\(dx\): differential notation
\(\frac{dy}{dx}\): derivative notation

12. Common calculus symbols

It is useful to recognise the notation that will appear later, even before studying each idea fully.

\(\lim\)
limit
\(\frac{d}{dx}\)
differentiate with respect to x
\(\int\)
integral
\(\partial\)
partial derivative symbol
\(\sum\)
summation
\(\infty\)
infinity

13. Read mathematics in layers

Complicated expressions become easier when read from their internal structure outward.

\(x\)
↓ add 1
\(x+1\)
↓ square
\((x+1)^2\)
↓ divide by 3
\(\frac{(x+1)^2}{3}\)

14. Context determines meaning

The same-looking symbol can mean different things in different settings. Mathematical notation must therefore be read together with its definition.

\(i\): an index, or \(\sqrt{-1}\)
\(e\): a variable, or Euler's number
\(P\): a point, probability, or matrix
meaning comes from context
Key takeaways

Notation is mathematical language. Read symbols together with their structure and definitions. Superscripts, subscripts, brackets, fractions and function notation each carry different information, while symbols such as \(\Delta x\), \(dx\), \(dy/dx\), \(\int\) and \(\partial\) prepare the language used throughout calculus.
