FOUNDATIONS 01

Number Systems

Different mathematical problems require different kinds of numbers. Number systems tell us which values are allowed and which calculations can be carried out.

Why number systems matter

Counting, debt, sharing, measurement and continuous change do not all use the same kind of number. Mathematics therefore extends the number system whenever the previous one is too limited.

\[ \mathbb N \subset \mathbb Z \subset \mathbb Q \subset \mathbb R \]

Each larger system keeps the earlier numbers but allows new kinds of problems to be solved.

1. Natural numbers

Natural numbers are the counting numbers:

\[ \mathbb N=\{1,2,3,4,\ldots\}. \]

Some definitions also include \(0\) in \(\mathbb N\).

Why they are needed: they describe quantities that are counted as whole objects. Examples include 25 students, 8 books, 3 infections or 120 customers. Fractions and negative values are usually not meaningful when counting individual objects.

2. Integers

Integers include positive whole numbers, negative whole numbers and zero:

\[ \mathbb Z=\{\ldots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\ldots\}. \]

Why they are needed: natural numbers cannot represent quantities below zero. Integers are used for temperatures below zero, financial gains and losses, floors below ground level, direction and changes such as \(-5\) or \(+3\).

3. Rational numbers

A rational number is any number that can be written as

\[ \frac{p}{q}, \qquad p,q\in\mathbb Z, \qquad q\neq0. \]

Examples include

\[ \frac12, \qquad -\frac34, \qquad 5=\frac51. \]

Why they are needed: integers cannot describe parts of a whole. Rational numbers allow proportions, percentages, rates and exact divisions, such as half a litre, \(2.5\) hours, a probability of \(3/4\), or a price of £1.25.

4. Rational decimals

Terminating and repeating decimals are rational:

\[ 0.5=\frac12, \qquad 0.333\ldots=\frac13. \]

Decimals are often more convenient than fractions for measurement, money, data and numerical computation, but mathematically they may represent exactly the same rational number.

5. Irrational numbers

An irrational number cannot be written as a fraction of two integers. Examples include

\[ \sqrt2, \qquad \pi, \qquad e. \]

Their decimal expansions do not terminate or eventually repeat.

Why they are needed: some exact quantities cannot be represented by rational numbers. For example, the diagonal of a unit square is \(\sqrt2\), the circumference of a circle involves \(\pi\), and continuous exponential growth naturally involves \(e\).

6. Real numbers

Real numbers include both rational and irrational numbers:

\[ \mathbb R = \{\text{rational numbers}\} \cup \{\text{irrational numbers}\}. \]

Every point on the ordinary number line represents a real number.

Why they are needed: real numbers describe quantities that vary continuously, such as time, distance, temperature, velocity and population proportions. Calculus is mainly developed on the real numbers because limits, derivatives and integrals require values to vary continuously rather than only in whole or fractional steps.

7. How the systems grow

Each extension solves a limitation of the previous system:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb N &: \text{counting},\\ \mathbb Z &: \text{counting plus negative values},\\ \mathbb Q &: \text{integers plus exact fractions},\\ \mathbb R &: \text{rational and irrational continuous values}. \end{aligned} \]

The important question is therefore not simply “what kind of number is this?” but also “what kind of quantity am I trying to represent?”
