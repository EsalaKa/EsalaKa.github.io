CHANGE AND LIMITS 01

What Change Means

Change compares a final value with an initial value.

The main idea

If a quantity changes from one value to another, its change is

\[ \text{change} = \text{final value} - \text{initial value}. \]

Change therefore describes a difference between two states of the same quantity.

1. A positive change

Suppose a quantity changes from \(4\) to \(9\).

\[ \text{change}=9-4=5. \]

The positive sign tells us that the quantity increased.

2. A negative change

Suppose the quantity changes from \(9\) to \(4\).

\[ \text{change}=4-9=-5. \]

The negative sign tells us that the quantity decreased.

3. Change is not the final value

If a value changes from \(10\) to \(13\), then

\[ \text{final value}=13, \]

but the change is only

\[ 13-10=3. \]

The value and the change in the value are different quantities.

4. The symbol \(\Delta\)

A finite change is commonly represented by the Greek capital letter \(\Delta\), read as “delta”.

If \(x\) changes from \(x_1\) to \(x_2\), then

\[ \Delta x=x_2-x_1. \]

Similarly, if \(y\) changes from \(y_1\) to \(y_2\),

\[ \Delta y=y_2-y_1. \]

5. Change depends on two values

The notation

\[ \Delta x \]

does not describe \(x\) itself. It describes the difference between two values of \(x\).

For example,

\[ x_1=2, \qquad x_2=2.1 \]

gives

\[ \Delta x=2.1-2=0.1. \]

6. Two quantities may change together

Suppose \(y\) depends on \(x\). When \(x\) changes, \(y\) may also change.

\[ x_1\rightarrow x_2 \]

causes

\[ y_1\rightarrow y_2. \]

We can therefore compare

\[ \Delta x \qquad\text{and}\qquad \Delta y. \]

7. From change to rate of change

Sometimes the size of \(\Delta y\) alone is not enough. We want to know how much \(y\) changed relative to the change in \(x\).

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}. \]

This is the average rate of change of \(y\) with respect to \(x\) over the interval.

8. Why this leads to calculus

Ordinary algebra compares two distinct values using finite changes.

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}. \]

Calculus asks what happens to this rate as the change in \(x\) becomes smaller and smaller.

\[ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{instantaneous rate of change}. \]

That idea will lead to limits and then to the derivative.
