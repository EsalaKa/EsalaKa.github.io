DIFFERENTIATION 06

The Product Rule

When two changing functions are multiplied, the change in their product depends on the change in both functions.

The main idea

Suppose

\[ y=f(x)g(x). \]

The derivative is not simply \(f'(x)g'(x)\).

Instead,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)g(x)] = f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x) } \]

This is the product rule.

1. Why do we need a special rule?

If both \(f(x)\) and \(g(x)\) change when \(x\) changes, then their product changes because of both changes.

For a small change in \(x\),

\[ f \rightarrow f+\Delta f \]

and

\[ g \rightarrow g+\Delta g. \]

The new product is

\[ (f+\Delta f)(g+\Delta g). \]

2. Expand the new product

Expanding gives

\[ fg+g\Delta f+f\Delta g+\Delta f\Delta g. \]

The original product was

\[ fg. \]

Therefore the change in the product is

\[ \Delta(fg) = g\Delta f + f\Delta g + \Delta f\Delta g. \]

This shows why both changing factors contribute to the total change.

3. Divide by the change in \(x\)

Divide by \(\Delta x\):

\[ \frac{\Delta(fg)}{\Delta x} = g\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} + f\frac{\Delta g}{\Delta x} + \frac{\Delta f\Delta g}{\Delta x}. \]

Rewrite the last term as

\[ \frac{\Delta f\Delta g}{\Delta x} = \Delta f \frac{\Delta g}{\Delta x}. \]

4. Take the limit

As

\[ \Delta x\to0, \]

for differentiable functions,

\[ \Delta f\to0. \]

Therefore,

\[ \Delta f \frac{\Delta g}{\Delta x} \to0. \]

The remaining terms give

\[ \boxed{ (fg)' = f'g+fg' } \]

which is the product rule.

5. A simple way to remember it

For

\[ y=fg, \]

differentiate one factor at a time:

\[ \boxed{ (fg)' = f'g + fg' } \]

First differentiate \(f\) and leave \(g\) unchanged. Then leave \(f\) unchanged and differentiate \(g\). Finally add the two results.

6. Example

Let

\[ y=x^2(x+3). \]

Take

\[ f(x)=x^2 \]

and

\[ g(x)=x+3. \]

Then

\[ f'(x)=2x \]

and

\[ g'(x)=1. \]

Using the product rule,

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = (2x)(x+3) + x^2(1). \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = 2x^2+6x+x^2. \]

So

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2+6x } \]

7. Check by expanding first

The original function is

\[ y=x^2(x+3). \]

Expanding,

\[ y=x^3+3x^2. \]

Differentiate:

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2+6x. \]

This agrees with the product rule.

8. Example where expanding is not convenient

Consider

\[ y=x^2\sin x. \]

Let

\[ f(x)=x^2, \qquad g(x)=\sin x. \]

Then

\[ f'(x)=2x, \qquad g'(x)=\cos x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = 2x\sin x+x^2\cos x } \]

The product rule is useful here because the product cannot be simplified by ordinary algebra before differentiating.

9. Example with an exponential function

Suppose

\[ y=xe^x. \]

Then

\[ f(x)=x, \qquad g(x)=e^x. \]

Their derivatives are

\[ f'(x)=1, \qquad g'(x)=e^x. \]

Therefore,

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = (1)e^x+x(e^x). \]

Hence,

\[ \boxed{ \frac{dy}{dx} = e^x(1+x) } \]

10. Three factors

The same idea extends to three functions.

If

\[ y=fgh, \]

differentiate one factor at a time:

\[ \boxed{ \frac{d}{dx}(fgh) = f'gh + fg'h + fgh' } \]

Each term contains exactly one differentiated factor.

11. A common mistake

Do not write

\[ (fg)'=f'g'. \]

For example,

\[ x^2x^3=x^5. \]

The correct derivative is

\[ 5x^4. \]

But multiplying the separate derivatives would give

\[ (2x)(3x^2)=6x^3, \]

which is different.

12. The structure to remember

\[ \boxed{ \text{first changes}\times\text{second} + \text{first}\times\text{second changes} } \]

In symbols,

\[ \boxed{ f'g+fg' } \]

The product changes because either factor can contribute to the change.
