CHANGE AND LIMITS 03

Average Rate of Change

Average rate of change measures how much a function changes in output for each unit of input change across a finite interval.

The main idea

For \(y=f(x)\), between \(x_1\) and \(x_2\),

\[\boxed{\text{average rate of change}=\frac{f(x_2)-f(x_1)}{x_2-x_1}=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}}.\]

Geometrically, this is the slope of the secant line through the corresponding two points on the curve.

1. Average rate on a curved function

Take \(f(x)=x^2\) from \(x=1\) to \(x=3\).

\[\Delta x=2,\qquad\Delta y=9-1=8,\]

so

\[\boxed{\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=4}.\]

The graph shows the curve, the two points, and the secant line whose slope is \(4\).

2. What the value means

The value \(4\) does not mean the slope of \(x^2\) is always \(4\). It means that over the chosen interval, the output increases on average by \(4\) units for each unit increase in \(x\).

A curved function can have different average rates on different intervals.

3. The geometric meaning

Let

\[P=(x_1,f(x_1)),\qquad Q=(x_2,f(x_2)).\]

The straight line through \(P\) and \(Q\) is the secant line, and

\[\boxed{\text{secant slope}=\frac{f(x_2)-f(x_1)}{x_2-x_1}}.\]

4. Difference quotient form

If the first input is \(x\) and the second is \(x+\Delta x\), then

\[\boxed{\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}{\Delta x}}.\]

This is called the difference quotient. It is the central algebraic expression that later becomes the derivative.

first point
\((x,f(x))\)

second point
\((x+\Delta x,f(x+\Delta x))\)

secant slope
\(\dfrac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}{\Delta x}\)

5. Difference quotient for \(f(x)=x^2\)

For \(f(x)=x^2\),

\[\frac{(x+\Delta x)^2-x^2}{\Delta x}.\]

Expanding and simplifying, for \(\Delta x\ne0\),

\[\boxed{\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=2x+\Delta x}.\]

This shows explicitly how the average rate depends on both the starting point and interval width.

\((x+\Delta x)^2-x^2\)
↓ expand
\(2x\Delta x+(\Delta x)^2\)
↓ divide by \(\Delta x\)
\(2x+\Delta x\)

6. Different intervals give different secant slopes

Fix the starting point at \(x=1\). For \(f(x)=x^2\):

\[x=1\to2:\quad\text{slope}=3,\]\[x=1\to3:\quad\text{slope}=4.\]

The graph compares these two secant lines on the same curve.

7. Making the interval smaller

At a fixed starting point \(x=2\),

\[\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=4+\Delta x.\]

As \(\Delta x\) becomes smaller, the average rate approaches \(4\):

\[5,\ 4.5,\ 4.1,\ 4.01,\ldots\]

8. Why we cannot simply set \(\Delta x=0\)

The difference quotient contains \(\Delta x\) in the denominator:

\[\frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}{\Delta x}.\]

Putting \(\Delta x=0\) directly would give division by zero. Calculus instead studies the value approached as

\[\boxed{\Delta x\to0}.\]
\(\Delta x\ne0\)
→ secant slope defined

\(\Delta x\to0\)
→ study the limiting value

do not substitute \(0\) first

9. From average rate to instantaneous rate

As the second point approaches the first, the secant line approaches a tangent line.

\[\text{average rate}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\text{instantaneous rate}.\]

This limiting slope is what the derivative measures.

Key takeaways

Average rate of change is the difference quotient \(\Delta y/\Delta x\), geometrically the slope of a secant line. For a curved function it depends on the chosen interval. Shrinking that interval leads to the limiting slope of the tangent line and therefore to the derivative.
