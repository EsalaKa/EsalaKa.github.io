Linear Functions

A linear function changes at a constant rate and has a straight-line graph.

The main idea

\[f(x)=mx+c.\]

The constant \(m\) gives the rate of change and \(c\) gives the output at \(x=0\).

1. The gradient

The number \(m\) is the gradient. For \(f(x)=3x+2\), increasing \(x\) by \(1\) increases \(f(x)\) by \(3\).

2. The intercept

The number \(c\) is the vertical intercept. For \(f(x)=3x+2\), the graph crosses the \(y\)-axis at \((0,2)\).

3. Constant rate of change

For two points, \(m=(y_2-y_1)/(x_2-x_1)\). The graph below makes the constant rise/run interpretation visible.

4. Example

For \(f(x)=2x+1\), the points \((1,3)\) and \((4,9)\) give \(\Delta x=3\), \(\Delta y=6\), so the gradient is \(2\).

5. Positive and negative gradient

A positive gradient rises from left to right; a negative gradient falls; zero gradient gives a horizontal line.

6. Finding a line from two points

For \((1,3)\) and \((4,9)\), \(m=2\). Substitution gives \(f(x)=2x+1\).

7. Linear models

Linear functions model constant change. For example, \(C(n)=5n+20\) can represent a fixed charge of \(20\) plus \(5\) per unit.

8. Why linear functions matter for calculus

A straight line has constant slope; calculus extends slope to curves using tangent lines.
