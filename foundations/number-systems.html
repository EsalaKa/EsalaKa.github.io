FOUNDATIONS 01

Number Systems

Numbers are extended step by step whenever an earlier system cannot represent what mathematics needs.

The main idea

We begin with numbers for counting, then add zero and negative values, fractions, irrational quantities, all real values, and finally complex numbers.

Each larger system contains the earlier one.

\[\boxed{\mathbb N\subset\mathbb Z\subset\mathbb Q\subset\mathbb R\subset\mathbb C}\]

Hierarchy of number systems

Each set is contained in the next:

ℕ⊂Natural numbers
ℤ⊂Integers
ℚ⊂Rational numbers
ℝ⊂Real numbers
ℂComplex numbers

1. Natural numbers \(\mathbb N\)

Used for counting. They start at \(1\) — or at \(0\), depending on convention — and continue without end.

\[\mathbb N=\{1,2,3,4,5,\ldots\}.\]

2. Integers \(\mathbb Z\)

Integers add zero and negative whole numbers, allowing us to represent values below zero.

\[\mathbb Z=\{\ldots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\ldots\}.\]

3. Rational numbers \(\mathbb Q\)

A rational number can be written as a fraction

\[\frac pq,\qquad p,q\in\mathbb Z,\ q\ne0.\]

There are infinitely many rational numbers between any two distinct rational numbers.

4. Irrational numbers

Irrational numbers cannot be written as a fraction of two integers. Examples include \(\sqrt2\), \(\pi\) and \(e\).

They are not outside the number line: they occupy ordinary real-number positions.

5. Real numbers \(\mathbb R\)

All rational and irrational numbers together form the real numbers.

Every point on the ordinary number line corresponds to exactly one real number.

6. Complex numbers \(\mathbb C\)

A complex number has the form

\[z=a+bi,\qquad a,b\in\mathbb R,\qquad i^2=-1.\]

Complex numbers add an imaginary direction, so they are represented on a plane rather than on a one-dimensional number line.

Key takeaways

Natural numbers count. Integers add negative values and zero. Rational numbers add exact fractions. Irrational and rational numbers together fill the real line. Complex numbers add a second, imaginary direction.

Why this matters for calculus

Most introductory calculus is developed on \(\mathbb R\), because quantities such as time, position and temperature can vary continuously. Later mathematics also studies calculus for complex-valued functions.
