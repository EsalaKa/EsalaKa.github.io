FUNCTIONS 13

Transformations of Functions

Transformations move, stretch, compress or reflect a graph while preserving its underlying structure.

The main idea

Starting with \(y=f(x)\), a transformed function can often be written as

\[\boxed{y=A f(B(x-C))+D}.\]

The constants outside \(f\) act vertically; the constants inside \(f\) act horizontally.

1. Vertical translation

Adding a constant outside the function changes every output:

\[y=f(x)+a.\]

If \(a>0\), the graph moves upward by \(a\). The graph compares \(y=x^2\) and \(y=x^2+3\).

2. Horizontal translation

Replacing \(x\) by \(x-a\) gives

\[y=f(x-a),\]

which shifts the graph right by \(a\). Thus \(y=(x-2)^2\) has vertex \((2,0)\), rather than \((0,0)\).

3. Vertical stretching and compression

Multiplying the whole function by \(A\) gives

\[y=Af(x).\]

If \(|A|>1\), vertical distances are stretched. If \(0<|A|<1\), they are compressed.

The graph compares \(x^2\), \(2x^2\), and \(\tfrac12x^2\).

4. Reflection in the \(x\)-axis

Multiplying all outputs by \(-1\) gives

\[y=-f(x).\]

Every point \((x,y)\) becomes \((x,-y)\). The graph compares \(y=x^2\) and \(y=-x^2\).

5. Reflection in the \(y\)-axis

Replacing the input by \(-x\) gives

\[y=f(-x).\]

Every point \((x,y)\) becomes \((-x,y)\). For example, \(e^{-x}\) is the reflection of \(e^x\) in the \(y\)-axis.

6. Horizontal stretching and compression

For

\[y=f(Bx),\]

the horizontal scale changes by a factor \(1/|B|\). Thus \(\sin(2x)\) completes twice as many cycles as \(\sin x\) over the same interval.

7. Several transformations together

Consider

\[y=2(x-3)^2+1.\]

Starting from \(y=x^2\): shift right by \(3\), stretch vertically by \(2\), then shift up by \(1\).

The new vertex is exactly \((3,1)\).

8. Why inside transformations look reversed

For \(y=f(x-2)\), the original input \(0\) is reached when

\[x-2=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad x=2.\]

So the feature originally at \(x=0\) appears at \(x=2\). This is why subtracting \(2\) inside shifts the graph right.

original input: \(0\)
↓
solve \(x-2=0\)
↓
new position: \(x=2\)

9. Reading the general transformation

In

\[y=A f(B(x-C))+D,\]

\(A\) controls vertical scale/reflection, \(B\) controls horizontal scale/reflection, \(C\) controls horizontal translation, and \(D\) controls vertical translation.

\(A\): vertical scale / reflection
\(B\): horizontal scale / reflection
\(C\): horizontal shift
\(D\): vertical shift

10. Why transformations matter for calculus

Transformations let us understand complicated graphs from familiar basic functions. They also help explain how parameters change derivatives, maxima, minima, roots and rates of change.

basic function
↓ transform
new graph
↓
predict position, scale and shape
Key takeaways

Outside changes act vertically and inside changes act horizontally. Translations move graphs, scale factors stretch or compress them, and negative factors produce reflections. Always identify exactly what happens to known points or features of the original graph.
