FUNCTIONS 09

Logarithmic Functions

A logarithm answers an exponent question: which power of a base produces a given positive number?

The main idea

For \(b>0\) and \(b\ne1\),

\[\boxed{\log_b x=y\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad b^y=x}.\]

The logarithm is therefore the inverse operation of exponentiation.

1. Reading a logarithm

Since

\[2^3=8,\]

we can write

\[\log_2 8=3.\]

So \(\log_2 8\) means “the power of \(2\) that gives \(8\)”.

\(2^3=8\)
⇕
\(\log_2 8=3\)

same relationship, read in reverse

2. Exponentials and logarithms are inverses

The functions \(y=e^x\) and \(y=\ln x\) undo one another.

Their graphs are mirror images across the line \(y=x\).

3. Domain of a logarithm

For real logarithms, the input must be positive:

\[\boxed{x>0}.\]

The curve \(y=\ln x\) approaches the vertical line \(x=0\) but never reaches or crosses it.

4. Important points on the graph

Every logarithmic function satisfies

\[\log_b1=0,\qquad \log_b b=1.\]

So \(y=\log_bx\) always passes through \((1,0)\) and \((b,1)\).

6. Logarithm laws

For positive \(x\) and \(y\),

\[\log_b(xy)=\log_bx+\log_by,\]\[\log_b\!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)=\log_bx-\log_by,\]\[\log_b(x^r)=r\log_bx.\]

These laws turn multiplication into addition, division into subtraction and powers into multiplication.

product → sum
quotient → difference
power → multiplier

7. The natural logarithm

The logarithm with base \(e\) is written

\[\boxed{\ln x=\log_e x}.\]

It is especially important in calculus because it is the inverse of \(e^x\).

\(e^y=x\)
⇕
\(y=\ln x\)

8. Inverse identities

Because \(e^x\) and \(\ln x\) undo one another,

\[\boxed{\ln(e^x)=x}\]

for every real \(x\), and

\[\boxed{e^{\ln x}=x}\]

for \(x>0\).

\(x\xrightarrow{e^x}e^x\xrightarrow{\ln}x\)

\(x\xrightarrow{\ln}\ln x\xrightarrow{e^{(\cdot)}}x\)

9. Solving exponential equations

Logarithms allow the unknown exponent to be brought down.

If

\[e^{2x}=7,\]

then

\[2x=\ln7,\qquad x=\frac{\ln7}{2}.\]
\(e^{2x}=7\)
↓ take \(\ln\)
\(2x=\ln7\)
↓
\(x=\ln7/2\)

10. Why \(\ln x\) matters for calculus

The natural logarithm has the derivative

\[\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}\ln x=\frac1x},\qquad x>0.\]

This connects logarithmic functions directly to rational functions and later to integration.

\(\ln x\)
↓ differentiate
\(1/x\)

logarithms ↔ rational functions
Key takeaways

A logarithm is an inverse exponent. Its real domain is \(x>0\), its graph has vertical asymptote \(x=0\), and \(\ln x\) is central to calculus because it is the inverse of \(e^x\) and has derivative \(1/x\).
