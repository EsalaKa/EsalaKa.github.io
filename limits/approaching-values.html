CHANGE AND LIMITS 05

Approaching a Value

To approach a value means to get arbitrarily close to it, without requiring equality.

The main idea

Consider the sequence

\[ 2.9,\qquad 2.99,\qquad 2.999,\qquad 2.9999,\qquad\ldots \]

These numbers are getting closer and closer to \(3\).

We describe this by saying that the values approach \(3\).

1. Approaching does not mean equal

The number

\[ 2.999 \]

is not equal to \(3\).

But its distance from \(3\) is only

\[ 3-2.999=0.001. \]

The value can be made as close to \(3\) as we wish.

2. The notation \(x\to a\)

The notation

\[ x\to a \]

is read as

“\(x\) approaches \(a\)”

or

“\(x\) tends to \(a\)”.

It describes what happens as \(x\) gets arbitrarily close to \(a\).

3. Approaching from the left

Values smaller than \(3\) can approach \(3\):

\[ 2.9,\qquad 2.99,\qquad 2.999,\qquad 2.9999,\qquad\ldots \]

They move toward \(3\) from the left side of the number line.

This is written

\[ x\to3^-. \]

The superscript \(-\) means approaching from values less than \(3\).

4. Approaching from the right

Values larger than \(3\) can also approach \(3\):

\[ 3.1,\qquad 3.01,\qquad 3.001,\qquad 3.0001,\qquad\ldots \]

They approach \(3\) from the right side.

This is written

\[ x\to3^+. \]

The superscript \(+\) means approaching from values greater than \(3\).

5. Approaching from both sides

When we simply write

\[ x\to3, \]

we normally consider values approaching \(3\) from both sides.

\[ 2.99 \longrightarrow 3 \longleftarrow 3.01 \]

The important question is what happens as the distance between \(x\) and \(3\) becomes arbitrarily small.

6. Distance is the important idea

The distance between \(x\) and \(a\) is

\[ |x-a|. \]

Therefore,

\[ x\to a \]

means that

\[ |x-a| \]

can be made as small as we wish.

This is a more precise way to understand the phrase “getting closer and closer.”

7. What happens to a function?

Now suppose

\[ f(x)=x^2. \]

Let \(x\) approach \(2\).

\[ 1.9,\qquad 1.99,\qquad 1.999,\qquad\ldots \]

The corresponding function values are

\[ 3.61,\qquad 3.9601,\qquad 3.996001,\qquad\ldots \]

They approach

\[ 4. \]

Similarly, approaching \(2\) from the other side,

\[ 2.1,\qquad 2.01,\qquad 2.001,\qquad\ldots \]

gives

\[ 4.41,\qquad 4.0401,\qquad 4.004001,\qquad\ldots \]

which also approach \(4\).

8. Input approaching and output approaching

We are now studying two things at the same time:

\[ x\to2 \]

and

\[ f(x)\to4. \]

In words:

\[ \boxed{ \text{as }x\text{ approaches }2, \quad f(x)\text{ approaches }4 } \]

This is the central idea behind a limit.

9. Why equality is not the main question

When studying a limit, we are interested in the behaviour of the function near a point.

The question is not initially

\[ \text{“What is }f(a)\text{?”} \]

but rather

\[ \text{“What does }f(x)\text{ approach as }x\to a\text{?”} \]

These questions are related, but they are not the same.

10. Returning to the secant line

In the previous lesson, two points \(P\) and \(Q\) determined a secant line.

We kept \(P\) fixed and moved \(Q\) closer to \(P\).

In terms of the horizontal separation,

\[ \Delta x\to0. \]

This does not mean that we immediately substitute

\[ \Delta x=0. \]

It means that we study what happens while \(\Delta x\) becomes arbitrarily small.

\[ \boxed{ \Delta x\to0 \quad\text{is not the same statement as}\quad \Delta x=0. } \]

11. The next step

We now have the idea needed to understand limit notation.

If \(f(x)\) approaches \(L\) as \(x\) approaches \(a\), we write

\[ \lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L. \]

The next lesson explains exactly what this notation means.
