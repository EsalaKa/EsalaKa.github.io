DIFFERENTIATION 03

Differentials: \(dx\) and \(dy\)

Differentials describe small linear changes and connect derivative notation with practical calculations.

The main idea

If \(y=f(x)\), then the derivative is

\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=f'(x)}\]

Once the derivative is known, the differential of \(y\) is defined by

\[\boxed{dy=f'(x)\,dx}\]

Here \(dx\) represents a small chosen change in the input, while \(dy\) is the corresponding linear change predicted by the derivative.

1. From finite changes to differentials

For a finite change \(\Delta x\), the exact change in the function is

\[\Delta y=f(x+\Delta x)-f(x).\]

For small \(\Delta x\), differentiability gives the approximation

\[\Delta y\approx f'(x)\Delta x.\]

The differential notation records the linear part of this change:

\[dy=f'(x)dx.\]

2. Example

Let \(y=x^2\). Then

\[\frac{dy}{dx}=2x.\]

Therefore

\[\boxed{dy=2x\,dx}\]

At \(x=3\), if \(dx=0.01\), then

\[dy=2(3)(0.01)=0.06.\]

So the derivative predicts a change of approximately \(0.06\) in \(y\).

3. Differential versus exact finite change

For the same example, the exact finite change is

\[\Delta y=(3.01)^2-3^2=0.0601.\]

The differential gives

\[dy=0.06.\]

Thus \(dy\) is the linear approximation to the actual finite change \(\Delta y\).

\[\boxed{\Delta y\approx dy\quad\text{for sufficiently small }\Delta x}\]

4. Is \(dy/dx\) a fraction?

The derivative \(dy/dx\) is defined through a limit, not originally as an ordinary fraction. However, after differentials are defined by \(dy=f'(x)dx\), the notation has a legitimate differential interpretation.

This explains why some fraction-like manipulations are valid when they are supported by rules such as the chain rule or separation of variables.

5. The chain rule

If \(y=f(u)\) and \(u=g(x)\), then

\[dy=\frac{dy}{du}\,du\]

and

\[du=\frac{du}{dx}\,dx.\]

Substituting gives

\[dy=\frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}\,dx,\]

so

\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}}\]

6. Why differentials are useful

Differentials are useful for linear approximation, error estimation, differential equations and later multivariable and stochastic calculus.

7. The central idea

\[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=f'(x)\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad dy=f'(x)dx}\]

The derivative gives the local rate of change; the differential uses that rate to describe a small linear change.
