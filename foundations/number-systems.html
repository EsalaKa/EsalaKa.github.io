FOUNDATIONS 01

Number Systems

Number systems grow as mathematics needs new kinds of numbers: first for counting, then negative values, fractions, continuous quantities and finally complex numbers.

The main idea

Each new number system extends the previous one. It keeps the earlier numbers but allows us to solve problems that were previously impossible.

\[\boxed{\mathbb N\subset\mathbb Z\subset\mathbb Q\subset\mathbb R\subset\mathbb C}\]

1. Natural numbers \(\mathbb N\): counting

Natural numbers are the counting numbers:

\[\mathbb N=\{1,2,3,4,\ldots\}.\]

They answer questions such as “How many?”: 4 books, 12 students or 100 trees. On a number line they appear as separate whole-number positions.

Some definitions include \(0\) in \(\mathbb N\). Natural numbers cannot represent quantities below zero or parts of a whole.

2. Integers \(\mathbb Z\): extending through zero

Suppose the temperature falls below \(0^\circ\), or a bank balance is below zero. We need negative numbers. This gives the integers:

\[\mathbb Z=\{\ldots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\ldots\}.\]

The line now extends indefinitely in both directions, but integers still cannot represent a value such as one half.

3. Rational numbers \(\mathbb Q\): filling the gaps

Division introduces numbers between the integers. A rational number can be written

\[\frac pq,\qquad p,q\in\mathbb Z,\quad q\ne0.\]

Zooming into only the interval from \(0\) to \(1\) already reveals many numbers:

Rational numbers include fractions, integers, terminating decimals and repeating decimals:

\[0.5=\frac12,\qquad0.75=\frac34,\qquad0.333\ldots=\frac13.\]

They are essential for proportions, probabilities, rates and measurements.

4. Irrational numbers: numbers fractions cannot represent

Even rational numbers are not enough. The diagonal of a unit square has exact length \(\sqrt2\), and \(\sqrt2\) cannot be written as a fraction of integers. Numbers of this kind are irrational.

Other examples include \(\pi\) and \(e\). Their decimal expansions do not terminate or eventually repeat.

Their positions above are determined by their numerical values. Irrational does not mean “off the number line”; it means “not expressible as a ratio of integers”.

5. Real numbers \(\mathbb R\): the complete number line

Rational and irrational numbers together form the real numbers:

\[\mathbb R=\mathbb Q\cup(\mathbb R\setminus\mathbb Q).\]

This continuous line is the natural setting for most elementary calculus. Time, distance and other continuously varying quantities are usually modelled using real numbers.

6. Why real numbers are still not enough

Consider

\[x^2+1=0.\]

This requires \(x^2=-1\), but no real number has a negative square. We therefore define

\[i^2=-1\]

and obtain the solutions \(x=\pm i\).

7. Complex numbers \(\mathbb C\)

A complex number has the form

\[z=a+bi,\qquad a,b\in\mathbb R.\]

The real number \(a\) and imaginary coefficient \(b\) require two coordinates, so complex numbers are represented on a plane, not on a one-dimensional number line.

The point \((2,3)\) represents \(2+3i\). Every real number is also complex: for example, \(5=5+0i\). Therefore \(\mathbb R\subset\mathbb C\).

8. The complete progression

The extensions can now be read as a sequence of mathematical needs:

\[\boxed{\underbrace{\mathbb N}_{\text{count}}\subset\underbrace{\mathbb Z}_{\text{negative values}}\subset\underbrace{\mathbb Q}_{\text{fractions}}\subset\underbrace{\mathbb R}_{\text{continuous line}}\subset\underbrace{\mathbb C}_{\text{complex plane}}}\]

Each extension solves equations or represents quantities that the previous system could not.

9. Why this matters for calculus

Most introductory calculus takes place on \(\mathbb R\), because variables such as time and distance vary continuously along the real number line. Later mathematics extends calculus to complex-valued functions as well.
