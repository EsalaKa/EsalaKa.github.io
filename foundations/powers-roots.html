Powers and Roots

Powers express repeated multiplication compactly; roots reverse powers and recover the quantity that produced them.

Why powers and roots matter

Many relationships are not linear. Areas involve squares, volumes involve cubes, population growth can involve exponential powers, and distance formulas naturally produce square roots.

\[\boxed{\text{length}\rightarrow\text{area }(x^2)\rightarrow\text{volume }(x^3)}\]

Powers therefore describe how quantities scale, while roots allow us to work backwards.

1. Positive integer powers

For a positive integer \(n\),

\[a^n=\underbrace{a\times a\times\cdots\times a}_{n\text{ factors}}.\]

In \(a^n\), \(a\) is the base and \(n\) is the exponent.

2. Multiplying powers

For the same base, factors combine:

\[a^ma^n=a^{m+n}.\]

For example, \(x^2x^3=x^5\).

3. Dividing powers

For \(a\ne0\),

\[\frac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}.\]

The exponent decreases because common factors cancel.

4. Power of a power

\[(a^m)^n=a^{mn}.\]

For example, \((x^2)^3\) contains three groups of two factors, giving \(x^6\).

5. Zero and negative powers

Consistency with the index laws gives

\[a^0=1,\qquad a^{-n}=\frac1{a^n},\qquad a\ne0.\]

A negative exponent therefore means reciprocal, not a negative value.

6. Roots as inverse operations

If \(b^n=a\), then

\[\sqrt[n]{a}=b\]

for the appropriate principal root. Thus roots answer the reverse question: “what value was raised to this power?”

7. Fractional powers

\[a^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a},\qquad a^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}\]

when defined in the real numbers. Fractional exponents connect powers and roots into one consistent notation.

8. A useful distinction

\(\sqrt9=3\) denotes the principal non-negative square root, but the equation \(x^2=9\) has two real solutions, \(x=3\) and \(x=-3\).

9. Connection to calculus

Power functions such as \(x^2\), \(x^{-1}\) and \(x^{1/2}\) appear throughout calculus. Understanding exponent notation makes differentiation and integration rules much more natural.
